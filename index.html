What you’re seeing

Marking ON. A path marker makes the two alternatives distinguishable in principle. The signal distribution is a smooth envelope with no fringes.

Erasure at +45°. With marking off, measuring the idler photon at +45° sorts out one subset of signal detections. That subset has bright and dark interference fringes.

Sorted by idler. Sorting detections into +45° and −45° subsets reveals two fringe patterns shifted by half a cycle. The two patterns complement each other.

Combined data. When both idler outcomes are added together, the fringes cancel and the overall distribution is flat. The unsorted signal data never reveal a fringe pattern.

The experiment, step by step

  1. What is a quantum eraser?

    It is an experiment that tests how interference changes when information that could distinguish alternative paths is available. “Erasing” means measuring in a way that no longer reveals which path; it does not mean changing the past.

  2. The setup

    A laser pumps a BBO crystal, which can create entangled signal and idler photons. The signal photon reaches a double slit, then a polarizer and detector. Its idler partner travels through an analyzer to a second detector.

  3. Why does the pattern disappear?

    When a path marker makes the alternatives distinguishable, their probability amplitudes do not produce visible interference. The signal detections form a smooth envelope.

  4. How does the pattern return?

    With marking removed, idler measurements at +45° or −45° sort the signal detections into subsets. Each subset shows fringes, with opposite phases; combining them removes the fringes.

  5. Can this send a faster-than-light message?

    No. A signal detector alone always shows the same fringe-free distribution. The matching idler results must be compared through ordinary communication, which cannot travel faster than light.

  6. The key idea

    Interference depends on whether the path is knowable in principle, not on whether anyone looked.
